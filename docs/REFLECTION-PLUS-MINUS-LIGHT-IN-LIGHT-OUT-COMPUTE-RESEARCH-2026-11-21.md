# REFLECTION± — Light In, Light Out, Compute
## Bidirectional Optical–Computational Systems Research

**Research date:** 21 November 2026  
**Status:** RESEARCH / SYNTHESIS / PROPOSED EXPERIMENTS  
**Owner:** IKAI research  
**Infrastructure responsibility:** WARENA  
**Shared evidence and terminology:** FELOH Corpus  
**Application contexts:** NAKEBUSEN Beauty Tech; GENJE embodied computing  
**Implementation status:** No integrated REFLECTION± system is claimed. This document records a research synthesis and proposed validation programme, not a product specification, procurement decision, implementation report or validated result.

> **Research proposition:** Light is acquired, transformed and returned through a coordinated optical–computational system. The system's output changes the conditions under which further input is received. The human participant remains an agent in this loop, not merely an object being measured.

## 1. Executive summary

REFLECTION± names a research programme for systems that connect optical input, computation and optical output:

**LIGHT IN → COMPUTE → LIGHT OUT → ENVIRONMENT / HUMAN RESPONSE → LIGHT IN**

The proposition extends IKAI's existing reflective-computing architecture. It does not replace the physics of a mirror with a metaphor. It distinguishes:

1. **Passive optical behaviour:** reflection, transmission, refraction, absorption and scattering.
2. **Optical acquisition:** sensors convert incident optical energy into measurable electrical signals.
3. **Digital computation:** processors transform sampled data and control output.
4. **Optical emission or modulation:** displays, projectors, LEDs, lasers or spatial light modulators produce or control outgoing light.
5. **Human/environment feedback:** people interpret the output and may act, changing the next observation.

The five operations may coexist in one installation, but they are physically and computationally distinct. A passive mirror does not compute. A camera is not a mirror. A display is not a reflective surface. A computational optical system coordinates these components and measures their combined behaviour.

The initial engineering strategy is **electronic-compute-first, native/local-first, standards-aware and measurement-led**. Photonic computing remains a comparative research track, not a prerequisite. Existing standards and mature components should be reused before proposing new interfaces or hardware.

### Main conclusions

- Computational optical imaging provides the strongest conceptual foundation for co-designing physical optics and digital reconstruction.
- Event-based vision is a relevant input modality for high-speed movement and low-latency interaction, but requires event-aware processing.
- MIPI CSI-2 v4.2 (adopted 15 December 2025) includes standardised protocol support for event-based vision sensor use cases; this does not mean every event camera implements CSI-2 or that IKAI hardware supports it.
- MIPI DSI-2 v2.2 is an established host-to-display interface; it is not a universal interface for every panel or projector.
- Linux already provides relevant interfaces and subsystems: V4L2, Media Controller, DRM/KMS, and established multimedia pipeline software such as GStreamer.
- IEEE 1588 PTP is a relevant network time-synchronisation standard. A shared clock does not by itself synchronise exposure, sensor readout, processing, display scanout or emitted photons.
- End-to-end latency must be measured as a distribution under a stated workload. A single FPS figure or average latency is insufficient.
- Photonic computing has promising properties for selected operations, but optical/electrical conversion, nonlinear operations, calibration, precision and system integration remain material constraints.
- The first demonstrator should establish useful reflection and controlled display output before optional sensing, tracking, XR or photonic acceleration are introduced.

## 2. Scope, definitions and conceptual model

### 2.1 What “light in” means

“Light in” is shorthand for optical energy arriving at a sensing or optical interface. Depending on the instrument, the system may measure intensity, spatial distribution, wavelength, polarisation, phase, time of flight or changes in brightness.

A sensor does not capture the complete physical scene. It samples a limited signal through an optical system, exposure regime, spectral response, geometry, dynamic range and noise profile.

### 2.2 What “light out” means

“Light out” includes light generated by an emitter or light whose properties are controlled by an optical modulator. Examples include emissive displays, backlit displays, projectors, LEDs, lasers and spatial light modulators.

A display output is not necessarily a faithful reconstruction of the physical scene. It is a designed visual signal with its own colour gamut, luminance, viewing angle, refresh behaviour, optical path and calibration requirements.

### 2.3 What “compute” means

Compute may occur electronically, optically, or in a hybrid system. It includes acquisition control, signal processing, reconstruction, sensor fusion, state management, rendering, feedback control, storage and device orchestration.

For the first IKAI experiments, conventional electronic computation is the baseline. Optical/photonic processing is assessed only where a workload-specific advantage can be demonstrated against that baseline.

### 2.4 Four optical/computational paths

| Path | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| LIGHT → LIGHT | Passive optical transformation | Mirror, lens, filter, diffuser |
| LIGHT → COMPUTE | Optical sensing and digitisation | Camera, photodiode, spectrometer |
| COMPUTE → LIGHT | Computation controls emission/modulation | Display, projector, LED controller |
| LIGHT ↔ COMPUTE ↔ LIGHT | Closed optical-computational interaction | Camera/display mirror, interactive projection |

The fourth path is the principal REFLECTION± research subject. The first three remain independently testable subsystems.

### 2.5 The meaning of ±

For this programme, ± denotes reciprocal direction and feedback, not a claim that optical signals have a universal positive/negative polarity.

- **INWARD / acquisition:** environment → sensor/system.
- **OUTWARD / expression:** system → display/optical environment.
- **RECIPROCAL / feedback:** output changes what a person or environment perceives and may change the next input.

This is a working research notation. It should not be confused with a formal mathematical operator or a recognised external standard.

## 3. System architecture

### 3.1 Layer model

| Layer | Responsibilities | Candidate technologies / methods |
|---|---|---|
| Optical scene | Illumination, reflectance, transmission, geometry | Mirror coatings, lenses, controlled lighting |
| Acquisition | Sampling and conversion | CMOS/CCD, event sensors, photodiodes, ToF, spectral sensors |
| Device transport | Carry sensor and control data | CSI-2, USB, GigE Vision, CoaXPress, PCIe, vendor interfaces |
| Time and synchronisation | Clocking, timestamps, trigger and alignment | Hardware trigger, device clocks, PTP/IEEE 1588, monotonic clocks |
| Compute | Processing and orchestration | CPU, GPU, DSP, FPGA; optional photonic accelerator |
| Rendering/output transport | Generate and deliver output frames/control | Native graphics APIs, DRM/KMS, DSI-2, DisplayPort, projector interfaces |
| Optical output | Emit or modulate light | LCD, OLED, microLED, DLP, LCoS, LED/laser, SLM |
| Human/environment interaction | Interpretation, action, consent and feedback | Direct controls, touch, movement, participant-led interaction |
| Governance | Permissions, retention, safety and accountability | Local policy, visible state, consent and deletion controls |

These are logical layers, not a mandated physical topology. A device may combine several layers. A system may be single-host or distributed.

### 3.2 Reference data path

1. An optical scene is illuminated by ambient or controlled light.
2. A sensor receives a portion of the optical signal through its lens/filter/optical path.
3. The sensor samples and digitises the signal, or emits an event stream.
4. A device interface transports data and associated metadata to the host.
5. The acquisition pipeline timestamps, validates and queues data.
6. Processing transforms or reconstructs the information.
7. A renderer creates an output representation.
8. The display pipeline schedules and transmits output.
9. The display or optical modulator emits or modulates light.
10. A person observes the output and decides whether to respond.
11. The environment and/or participant action changes the next acquisition.

The loop includes human interpretation and choice. It must not be modelled as automatic behavioural optimisation or hidden surveillance.

### 3.3 Separate optical reflection from camera/display simulation

A physical mirror returns light from the scene. A camera/display system samples an image and emits a new image. Their geometry, colour response, latency, perspective, dynamic range and optical path differ.

A digital “mirror” may therefore exhibit:
- camera viewpoint offset;
- image reversal or non-reversal choices;
- exposure and rolling-shutter artefacts;
- display refresh and scanout delay;
- colour and white-balance differences;
- cropping, distortion and lens effects;
- display contrast loss through reflective glass;
- temporal mismatch between physical and digital representations.

The physical reflection remains the reference in IKAI's initial beauty/loc-care context. A camera feed must not silently replace it.

## 4. LIGHT IN — optical acquisition research

### 4.1 Sensor families

| Sensor family | Primary measurement | Useful properties | Limitations / questions |
|---|---|---|---|
| CMOS image sensor | Spatial intensity samples | Mature, high-resolution, broad availability | Exposure, rolling shutter, noise, spectral response, bandwidth |
| CCD image sensor | Photocharge accumulated per pixel | Established scientific imaging history | Power, readout architecture, availability and integration vary |
| Event-based vision | Asynchronous brightness-change events | High temporal resolution, high dynamic range, reduced motion blur in suitable scenes | Does not directly provide conventional intensity frames; event processing and reconstruction are specialised |
| Time-of-flight depth | Optical travel-time-derived depth | Direct depth estimates within sensor limits | Multipath, ambient light, range and material effects |
| LiDAR | Range from emitted/received light | Spatial ranging and mapping | Cost, eye-safety, reflectivity, environment and integration |
| Photodiode | Optical intensity at detector | Fast, simple, useful for timing and light measurement | Limited spatial information unless arrayed/scanned |
| Spectrometer | Spectral distribution | Material/colour investigation | Calibration, acquisition time, optical complexity |
| Polarisation sensor | Polarisation state or contrast | Surface/material cues in specialised contexts | Specialised hardware and interpretation |

### 4.2 Event-based vision

Event cameras differ from frame cameras. Instead of reporting a complete image at a fixed frame rate, each pixel asynchronously reports brightness changes, typically with location, timestamp and polarity.

The 2022 TPAMI survey by Gallego et al. describes event cameras' high temporal resolution, high dynamic range and low-latency potential, alongside the need for algorithms suited to their event-stream representation. These properties make event-based sensing relevant to GENJE movement studies and fast interaction, but do not establish that it is automatically superior for beauty imaging or ordinary mirror preview.

**Primary review:** Gallego et al., “Event-Based Vision: A Survey,” *IEEE Transactions on Pattern Analysis and Machine Intelligence*, 44(1), 154–180 (2022), DOI: [10.1109/TPAMI.2020.3008413](https://doi.org/10.1109/TPAMI.2020.3008413).

**Interface development:** MIPI CSI-2 v4.2 was adopted on 15 December 2025 and adds standardised protocol support for event-based vision sensor use cases through Event Sensing and Processing (ESP). This is a standards development, not evidence of universal device availability or IKAI implementation. See [MIPI CSI-2](https://www.mipi.org/specifications/csi-2) and the [MIPI specification version history](https://www.mipi.org/hubfs/Specification-Feature-Tables/MIPI-CSI-2-Version-History-Table-March-2026.pdf).

### 4.3 Acquisition variables

Every camera/sensor experiment should record:
- sensor and lens make/model, firmware and driver;
- sensor resolution, pixel format and bit depth;
- frame rate or event rate;
- exposure time and gain;
- shutter type and readout mode;
- white balance and colour processing;
- lens focal length, aperture, focus and distortion calibration;
- illumination spectrum, illuminance and geometry;
- sensor timestamp source and clock domain;
- transport interface and negotiated link mode;
- buffer count, queue depth, dropped data and error counters;
- ambient conditions and test duration.

Do not report “camera quality” without a task, measurement method and conditions.

## 5. LIGHT OUT — display and optical-output research

### 5.1 Output technologies

| Technology | Output principle | Research relevance | Key trade-offs |
|---|---|---|---|
| LCD | Liquid-crystal modulation of a backlight | Practical initial rear-display candidate | Black level, viewing angle, backlight, heat, panel construction |
| OLED | Self-emissive pixels | Contrast and black-level control | Burn-in/ageing, brightness, cost, panel transparency variants |
| MicroLED | Inorganic micro-scale emitters | High-brightness and modular-display research | Manufacturing, yield, repairability, cost |
| DLP | Digital micromirror array modulates projected light | Projection and spatial light modulation | Optical path, noise, brightness, focus, alignment |
| LCoS | Liquid-crystal-on-silicon reflective modulation | Projection and optical systems | Polarisation/optical train, alignment and brightness |
| Laser projection | Laser sources generate projected imagery | Large or spatially mapped output | Speckle, safety, optics and calibration |
| Spatial light modulator | Controls amplitude/phase or other wavefront properties depending on device | Holography, adaptive optics and optical processing | Device-specific resolution, speed, phase range and calibration |

No single display technology is universally best. Selection depends on the optical assembly, room lighting, viewing distance, thermal envelope, brightness, repairability and cost.

### 5.2 Display interfaces

[MIPI DSI-2](https://www.mipi.org/specifications/dsi-2) defines a high-bandwidth link between host processors and display modules. The MIPI page lists DSI-2 v2.2 (July 2024) as its current version and describes support for D-PHY, C-PHY and, for longer-reach applications, A-PHY physical layers. The specification is member-access controlled; public summaries do not substitute for the complete normative specification.

[DisplayPort](https://www.displayport.org/) is another relevant display transport family for host-to-display connectivity. The actual interface must be selected from the chosen compute/display hardware rather than assumed.

### 5.3 Reflective surfaces and transparent displays

The existing IKAI mirror research distinguishes:
- partially reflective / partially transmissive mirror glass with a separate rear display;
- camera-and-screen digital mirror;
- augmented-reality mirror with registered digital objects;
- transparent emissive display such as transparent OLED;
- mixed-reality reflected environment.

These are separate engineering configurations.

For a partially reflective surface, the visible result depends on the optical properties of the coating and substrate, illumination on both sides, display luminance, viewing angle, ambient reflections, display-to-glass spacing and enclosure geometry. “Two-way mirror” is a common product description, not a complete optical specification.

A transparent OLED is an emissive display with a transparent optical path; it is not equivalent to partially reflective mirror glass. Neither technology should be assumed to preserve a natural reflection under all lighting conditions.

**Required measurement:** test the assembled optical stack, not just a glass datasheet or display datasheet in isolation.

Measure or document:
- spectral reflectance and transmittance where suitable instruments/data are available;
- front- and rear-side illuminance;
- display luminance and contrast through the assembled surface;
- haze, tint, ghosting, double images and distortion;
- viewing-angle behaviour;
- display-off, idle and maximum intended brightness conditions;
- thermal behaviour after warm-up;
- appearance against a conventional reference mirror.

## 6. COMPUTE — electronic, heterogeneous and photonic

### 6.1 Electronic baseline

| Compute resource | Appropriate role | Risks / limits |
|---|---|---|
| CPU | Control, orchestration, general-purpose processing | Parallel throughput depends on workload |
| GPU | Image processing, reconstruction, rendering and parallel workloads | Memory transfer, driver stack, power and thermal load |
| DSP | Signal-processing pipelines | Vendor-specific toolchains and supported operations |
| FPGA | Deterministic/custom pipelines, interface bridging and low-latency processing | Development complexity, timing closure, toolchain openness |
| ASIC | High-volume fixed-function processing | Design cost, inflexibility and fabrication constraints |

The first demonstrator should use a conventional local compute host. Benchmark the complete pipeline before deciding whether a GPU, DSP or FPGA is necessary.

### 6.2 Photonic computing

Photonic computing uses optical signals to perform selected information-processing operations. Research includes free-space optics, integrated photonic circuits, coherent and incoherent optical processing, optical neural networks and neuromorphic photonics.

Two important reviews:
- Farmakidis, Dong & Bhaskaran, “Integrated photonic neuromorphic computing: opportunities and challenges,” *Nature Reviews Electrical Engineering* 1, 358–373 (2024), [DOI 10.1038/s44287-024-00050-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44287-024-00050-9).
- Fu et al., “Optical neural networks: progress and challenges,” *Light: Science & Applications* 13, 263 (2024), [DOI 10.1038/s41377-024-01590-3](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41377-024-01590-3).

The reviews describe potential advantages in parallel optical operations and throughput, but also identify major engineering issues:
- optical/electrical conversion and its overhead;
- implementation of nonlinear operations;
- optical loss and amplification;
- precision and calibration;
- dynamic reconfiguration;
- time-domain processing;
- integration and packaging;
- memory and data movement;
- scalable cascaded operation.

**Decision:** photonic compute is an EXPERIMENTAL research track. Do not assume that it reduces total system latency or energy. Any comparison must include sensors, modulators, lasers, detectors, conversion, control electronics, memory, packaging and workload-specific accuracy.

### 6.3 Computational optical imaging

Computational optical imaging jointly designs physical optical acquisition and digital algorithms. Instead of treating the lens/sensor as a finished image-forming device and software as a later enhancement, the optical system and reconstruction algorithm are co-designed.

Wang, Peng, Fang & Gao, “Computational optical imaging: on the convergence of physical and digital layers,” *Optica* 12(1), 113 (17 January 2025), is a central review. [Open article PDF](https://hub.hku.hk/bitstream/10722/361862/1/content.pdf?accept=1).

Research families include:
- coded-aperture imaging;
- light-field imaging;
- computational photography;
- computational microscopy;
- phase retrieval and wavefront reconstruction;
- computational holography;
- inverse rendering and scene reconstruction;
- learned and model-based reconstruction.

The research programme should distinguish measured data from reconstructed estimates. An algorithmic reconstruction is not direct ground truth; report assumptions, calibration, uncertainty and failure cases.

### 7.1 Standards map

|---|---|---|
| MIPI CSI-2 v4.2 | Sensor-to-host image/event transport | ESTABLISHED standard; device support must be checked |
| MIPI DSI-2 v2.2 | Host-to-display transport | ESTABLISHED standard; panel support must be checked |
| MIPI C-PHY / D-PHY / A-PHY | Physical layers used by MIPI interfaces | ESTABLISHED families; version compatibility matters |
| IEEE 1588-2019 PTP | Precision time synchronisation over packet networks | ESTABLISHED standard; profile/network/hardware support required |
| GenICam / GenTL | Generic industrial camera programming and transport abstraction | ESTABLISHED standard family; version and device compliance must be checked |
| GigE Vision | Ethernet-based machine-vision camera interface family | ESTABLISHED; exact camera/network implementation matters |
| USB3 Vision | USB machine-vision interface family | ESTABLISHED; exact host/device support matters |
| V4L2 | Linux userspace video interface | ESTABLISHED Linux API |
| Media Controller | Linux media graph configuration for supported devices | ESTABLISHED Linux API |
| DRM/KMS | Linux display/modesetting subsystem | ESTABLISHED Linux subsystem |
| GStreamer | Media pipeline framework with clocking, timestamps and latency handling | ESTABLISHED software; not a hardware interface standard |
| OpenXR | Cross-vendor XR application API and frame timing | ESTABLISHED API; runtime/device support is specific |

**MIPI CSI-2:** [official specification overview](https://www.mipi.org/specifications/csi-2). The current public page lists v4.2, adopted 15 December 2025. The full specification is available to MIPI members.

**MIPI DSI-2:** [official specification overview](https://www.mipi.org/specifications/dsi-2). The current public page lists v2.2 (July 2024).

**IEEE 1588:** [IEEE 1588-2019 official record](https://standards.ieee.org/ieee/1588/6825/). It defines precision clock synchronisation for packet-based networked measurement/control systems. IEEE describes sub-microsecond synchronisation and notes that sub-nanosecond transfer accuracy can be achieved in a properly designed network. These are not guarantees for an arbitrary installation.

**GenICam:** [EMVA overview](https://www.emva.org/standards-technology/genicam/) and [downloads/version records](https://www.emva.org/standards-technology/genicam/genicam-downloads/). GenICam aims to provide a common programming interface across camera interface technologies. Its modules include GenApi, GenTL, SFNC, PFNC and others. The public downloads page lists a GenICam package version 2026.07; record exact component versions when evaluating a device.

### 7.2 Linux native interfaces

The Linux kernel documents:
- [Video for Linux API (V4L2)](https://docs.kernel.org/userspace-api/media/v4l/v4l2.html), including video capture and device configuration;
- [V4L2 capture interface](https://docs.kernel.org/userspace-api/media/v4l/dev-capture.html);
- [DRM/KMS](https://docs.kernel.org/gpu/drm-kms.html), including atomic modesetting and display pipeline state.

These are existing kernel interfaces. They do not guarantee that a particular camera, bridge, GPU, panel or driver supports every capability.

### 7.3 GStreamer and time-aware pipelines

GStreamer documents live-source timestamps, pipeline clocks, running-time, latency queries and latency compensation:
- [Clocks and synchronisation](https://gstreamer.freedesktop.org/documentation/application-development/advanced/clocks.html)
- [Latency design](https://gstreamer.freedesktop.org/documentation/additional/design/latency.html)
- [Synchronisation design](https://gstreamer.freedesktop.org/documentation/additional/design/synchronisation.html)

This makes GStreamer a useful candidate for comparative pipeline experiments. It is not automatically the final IKAI runtime and should be evaluated against native direct interfaces and the actual workload.

### 7.4 OpenXR frame timing

The Khronos OpenXR frame loop includes xrWaitFrame, xrBeginFrame and xrEndFrame. The runtime returns predictedDisplayTime, a prediction of when the next frame is expected to be displayed. This is important for XR and spatial output, but it is not a substitute for measuring physical sensor-to-photon latency in a mirror or projection installation.

Reference: [Khronos OpenXR Guide — Frame Submission](https://github.com/KhronosGroup/OpenXR-Guide/blob/main/chapters/frame_submission.md) and [OpenXR specification — rendering](https://registry.khronos.org/OpenXR/specs/1.1/html/xrspec.html).

## 8. Timing, latency and performance engineering

### 8.1 End-to-end latency

A useful decomposition is:

T_total = T_exposure/readout + T_transport + T_queue + T_process + T_render + T_output_scanout + T_optical_response

For a movement-driven system, add the latency between physical movement onset and the sensor's first usable observation. For an interactive control path, separately measure input-to-visible-response.

The terms are not always strictly serial: pipelines may overlap capture, processing and display. The equation is an accounting model, not an assumption that all stages execute sequentially.

### 8.2 Report distributions, not a single number

For each test, report:
- median latency;
- p95 and p99 latency;
- maximum observed latency with test duration;
- jitter / variation;
- dropped frames or events;
- queue depth and buffer occupancy;
- missed presentation deadlines;
- throughput and sustained frame/event rate;
- recovery after overload or device interruption.

Report FPS and latency separately. A stable 60 FPS pipeline can still have substantial capture-to-photon delay.

### 8.3 Synchronisation is not one problem

Distinguish:
1. **Clock synchronisation:** devices share or relate timestamps to a common time base.
2. **Exposure synchronisation:** sensors begin exposure at a controlled relative time.
3. **Readout synchronisation:** data is sampled/transferred with known timing.
4. **Processing synchronisation:** multiple streams are associated with the correct temporal state.
5. **Presentation synchronisation:** output is submitted/presented at a controlled display time.
6. **Optical observation:** actual emitted photons become visible at the physical surface.

PTP can help with network clock alignment. Hardware trigger lines may be required for exposure alignment. A display's scanout and pixel response introduce further timing. Each stage must be measured.

### 8.4 Motion-to-photon measurement

A 2022 peer-reviewed study presents a system-independent, high-speed-camera method to measure motion-to-photon latency through co-registering real and displayed controller movement: [Measuring motion-to-photon latency for sensorimotor experiments with virtual reality systems](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10616216/).

For REFLECTION±, adapt the principle to a test fixture:
- capture a physical LED/marker or mechanical movement in the same high-speed recording as the display response;
- define a reproducible input onset;
- define the corresponding visible output event;
- measure across multiple repetitions and motion phases;
- account for camera exposure, rolling shutter, display scanout and marker threshold;
- publish raw measurement methodology and uncertainty.

Do not claim a target latency until the use case, measurement method and hardware are defined.

## 9. Proposed experimental programme

All experiments below are **PROPOSED**. No hardware has been selected, purchased, assembled or validated by this document.

### E0 — Requirements and baseline

**Question:** What is the minimum useful optical-computational loop for each application?

- Define one task per context.
- Identify the minimum input and output channels.
- Identify whether camera sensing is actually required.
- Set data and privacy boundaries before collection.
- Establish a conventional mirror and display-only baseline.
- Define the test room, geometry and available instruments.

**Deliverable:** signed-off test plan and requirement matrix; no participant data required.

### E1 — Passive optical assembly

**Question:** Can a selected partially reflective surface preserve useful reflection while transmitting enough display light?

- Compare candidate mirror samples against a conventional mirror.
- Record front/rear illuminance and display luminance.
- Test multiple observer angles and distances.
- Inspect tint, haze, ghosting, double reflection and distortion.
- Test display-off and display-on conditions.
- Repeat after thermal warm-up.

**Measurements:** reflectance/transmittance where measurable, illuminance, luminance, viewing geometry, qualitative task success and thermal conditions.

**Pass condition:** agreed reflection tasks remain usable under the defined test conditions, with results documented. No universal optical threshold is assumed in advance.

### E2 — Display-through-surface output

**Question:** Can a display present legible information without compromising the primary reflection?

- Compare LCD and OLED candidates where available.
- Measure output luminance through the assembled optical surface.
- Evaluate contrast under low, nominal and high ambient illumination.
- Measure panel temperature and power under sustained operation.
- Evaluate viewing-angle and content placement.

**Pass condition:** predefined information can be read in the target zone while reflection remains the primary reference. Record all environmental conditions.

### E3 — Camera acquisition (optional and separately gated)

**Question:** Does a selected camera produce useful data in the assembled optical environment?

- Begin with a static non-identifying calibration target.
- Record lens, exposure, frame rate, pixel format and illumination.
- Compare direct camera view and through-surface view if relevant.
- Evaluate glare, reflections, flare, occlusion and colour shift.
- Verify camera-off state and visible capture indicator.

**Pass condition:** the selected task can be completed with stated error bounds and no unreported data path. No face recognition, biometric matching or diagnostic inference.

### E4 — Local compute and rendering loop

**Question:** Can local compute process acquired data and produce controlled output at a predictable rate?

- Capture using a supported native interface.
- Timestamp data at the earliest available point.
- Record buffer copies, queue depth and processing time.
- Render a simple transform/overlay.
- Record display submission and presentation timing where available.
- Test sustained operation and overload behaviour.

**Pass condition:** repeatable operation with published latency distributions, dropped-data counts, hardware/software versions and failure cases.

### E5 — Closed-loop interaction

**Question:** How does system output affect subsequent sensing and participant interaction?

- Use a non-identifying moving target or controlled light source first.
- Compare direct optical reflection with computed display feedback.
- Introduce a controlled visual output that changes the scene only when necessary.
- Measure oscillation, feedback instability, false triggering and temporal lag.
- Include a participant-controlled stop and a non-sensing fallback.

**Pass condition:** the system behaves predictably under the defined loop; participant intent remains explicit and the loop can be stopped immediately.

### E6 — Event-based sensing comparison

**Question:** Does event-based sensing improve a specific movement or interaction task over a frame camera?

- Use the same controlled motion and lighting.
- Compare event and frame sensor acquisition.
- Compare temporal resolution, motion blur, event/frame reconstruction, data volume and processing load.
- Report conditions where the event camera performs worse.
- Do not compare unlike devices without recording their optical and temporal settings.

**Pass condition:** a task-specific benefit is measured, not inferred from headline sensor specifications.

### E7 — Multi-device / distributed I/O

**Question:** Can multiple optical input and output devices remain temporally and spatially coherent?

- Start with two input streams and one output.
- Add shared timestamps and hardware triggers where supported.
- Test networked synchronisation only after local baselines.
- Measure inter-stream skew, packet loss, queue growth and recovery.
- Expand device count gradually.

**Pass condition:** synchronisation and performance remain within application-defined tolerances under sustained load. Do not invent a universal tolerance.

### E8 — Photonic-compute evaluation

**Question:** Does an available photonic processor improve a defined operation when the entire system is counted?

- Choose a workload with a clear numerical baseline.
- Compare accuracy, throughput, end-to-end latency and energy.
- Include optical source, modulation, detection, conversion and control overhead.
- Evaluate calibration drift and repeatability.
- Compare with CPU/GPU/FPGA implementation on the same task.

**Pass condition:** a measured end-to-end advantage for the selected workload. A component-level optical speed claim is insufficient.

- device abstraction and fault recovery;
- open standards, licensing and upstream reuse.

Do not introduce a new ABI, daemon, protocol or runtime until existing interfaces have been audited against measured requirements. Preserve the native-first, libre-first and local-first direction; do not use JavaScript or a browser engine as the application/runtime substrate.

### 10.4 GENJE — embodied computing

GENJE owns creative and movement practice.

Potential loop:
**movement → sensing → representation → transformation → sound/visual output → human response**

Research should compare frame cameras, event cameras, inertial sensing and existing motion-capture systems. Movement representations must preserve performer agency, context, provenance and uncertainty. Do not infer identity, emotion, personality, health or capability from movement data. Laban-related descriptors and any proposed movement IR remain research constructs until validated with practitioners.

### Research-gap discipline

A gap is not established merely because a feature is absent from one project or product. Before claiming novelty:
1. search scholarly literature and patents;
2. inspect relevant standards and vendor documentation;
3. identify existing open-source implementations;
4. inspect relevant hardware and product systems;
5. compare the actual use case and constraints;
6. record prior creators and licences;
7. state the remaining gap narrowly.

Reuse, adapt, interoperate and credit before inventing.

## 12. Evidence and maturity labels

Use these labels consistently:

| Label | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ESTABLISHED | A scientific principle, standard, method or technology is supported by authoritative evidence |
| IMPLEMENTED | A specific implementation exists; identify project, version/commit and scope |
| EXPERIMENTAL | A prototype, laboratory demonstration or active research result exists; record its limits |
| PROPOSED | A design, architecture, experiment or hypothesis is suggested but not implemented/validated |
| UNKNOWN | Evidence is insufficient, inaccessible or not yet checked |

These labels describe different dimensions. A technology can be ESTABLISHED as a field and still UNKNOWN in a particular hardware configuration. A software library can be IMPLEMENTED upstream while remaining untested in IKAI.

For project-specific claims, pair maturity with evidence:
- VERIFIED;
- CORROBORATED;
- DISPUTED;
- UNVERIFIED;
- INTERPRETATION;
- OPINION;
- TESTIMONY;
- PROPOSAL.

A repository commit proves a file exists on a branch. It does not prove hardware works. A vendor datasheet does not prove assembled-system performance. A literature result does not validate a new implementation.

## 13. Privacy, safety and participant authority

Before any collection involving people, document:
- purpose and research question;
- participant/data source;
- exact fields and modalities collected;
- whether data is identifiable or sensitive;
- processing location and all components with access;
- consent/notice and withdrawal method;
- retention and deletion procedure;
- access and export rules;
- security risks and misuse cases;
- validation and stop criteria.

Requirements:
- no face identification or biometric identity matching;
- no person scoring, ranking or attractiveness scoring;
- no diagnosis or clinical claims;
- no emotion/personality/health inference from appearance or movement;
- no covert recording or hidden profiling;
- no default cloud profile or undisclosed external transfer;
- camera and microphone remain visibly disabled unless the stated task requires them;
- preserve a useful non-camera path;
- use synthetic or non-identifying test targets whenever sufficient;
- never commit participant recordings or identifiable sensor data to the repository.

A written privacy intention is not a privacy audit. Verify actual data paths, permissions, retention and deletion in an implementation before making a privacy-preserving claim.

## 17. Conclusion

REFLECTION± is best treated as a research framework for **bidirectional optical–computational systems**.

The central engineering problem is not simply capturing an image and displaying it. It is coordinating optical behaviour, sensing, computation, time, output and human agency into a measurable system.

The strongest near-term path is:
1. establish passive optical behaviour;
2. validate display output through the selected optical assembly;
3. qualify optional sensing;
4. benchmark local computation and display timing;
5. close the loop under controlled conditions;
6. scale to multiple devices only when measurements justify it;
7. compare photonic computation against an electronic baseline only for a workload where it may matter.

**LIGHT IN · LIGHT OUT · COMPUTE.**  
**REFLECTION± — from passive reflection to measured computational reciprocity.**
