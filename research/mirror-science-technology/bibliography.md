# MIRROR — Annotated Master Bibliography (Starter Register)

**Status:** RESEARCH / CURATED STARTER SET  
**Last reviewed:** 2026-10-25  
**Purpose:** Traceable starting point, not an exhaustive bibliography. Recheck versions and source details before citing in a new study.

## A. Scientific method and research integrity

### A1. National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2019)
**Reproducibility and Replicability in Science.** Consensus study report.  
https://doi.org/10.17226/25303 · https://www.nationalacademies.org/read/25303  
**Relevance:** Definitions and analysis of reproducibility and replicability, computational methods, transparency, and factors that complicate independent confirmation.  
**Use:** Establish vocabulary and reporting requirements for computational research.  
**Caution:** Preserve the report's distinctions; terminology varies across fields.

### A2. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — “Scientific Method”
Scholarly reference article; first published 2015, substantive revision dated 8 August 2026 in the version surfaced during this research.  
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/scientific-method/  
**Relevance:** Surveys observation, experimentation, reasoning, models, hypothesis testing, and pluralism about scientific methods.  
**Use:** Avoid presenting one simplistic universal procedure as the only scientific method.  
**Caution:** A synthesis, not a substitute for primary texts or discipline-specific methods.

### A3. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — “Karl Popper”
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/popper/  
**Relevance:** Explains falsifiability, critical testing, corroboration, and the methodological complexities of evaluating counterevidence.  
**Use:** Make hypotheses testable and state what evidence could count against them.  
**Caution:** Falsifiability is influential but not a complete description of all scientific practice.

### A4. Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy — “Science and Pseudo-Science”
https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/pseudo-science/  
**Relevance:** Reviews the demarcation problem and competing approaches.  
**Use:** Support careful appraisal of methods and claims.  
**Caution:** Do not use labels as substitutes for analysing evidence, scope, and methods.

## B. Open science, data, and provenance

### B1. Wilkinson, M. D. et al. (2016). “The FAIR Guiding Principles for scientific data management and stewardship.” Scientific Data 3, 160018.
DOI: https://doi.org/10.1038/sdata.2016.18  
Open text: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4792175/  
**Relevance:** Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable principles for data and research objects, including metadata, provenance, identifiers, and licensing.  
**Use:** Design source records, metadata, and research-artefact reuse.  
**Caution:** FAIR does not require every dataset to be publicly accessible. Sensitive information may need controlled access; FAIR itself does not mandate one implementation.

### B2. UNESCO (2021). Recommendation on Open Science.
https://www.unesco.org/en/legal-affairs/recommendation-open-science  
Implementation overview: https://www.unesco.org/en/open-science/implementation  
**Relevance:** An international policy framework for accessibility, inclusion, cooperation, and reducing science and technology gaps.  
**Use:** Research governance, equitable participation, and capacity-building.  
**Caution:** Policy language does not itself prove that a particular intervention is effective or locally appropriate.

### B3. Git documentation
Official documentation: https://git-scm.com/doc  
**Relevance:** Version history can preserve authored changes, review context, and recoverable project states.  
**Use:** Provenance, reviewable changes, and project snapshots.  
**Caution:** A commit proves that a versioned change was recorded; it does not prove the truth of a factual claim or test result.

## C. AI risk and human accountability

### C1. NIST, Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, 2023)
https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework  
Resource centre: https://airc.nist.gov/airmf-resources/airmf/  
**Relevance:** A voluntary framework for managing AI risks across design, development, use, and evaluation.  
**Use:** AI research plans, risk registers, and evaluation questions.  
**Caution:** NIST indicates AI RMF 1.0 is being revised. Record the version used and check the official site for updates.

### C2. NIST Special Publication 1270 (2022), Towards a Standard for Identifying and Managing Bias in Artificial Intelligence
https://doi.org/10.6028/NIST.SP.1270  
**Relevance:** Examines bias in technical and wider social contexts.  
**Use:** Dataset limitation analysis, stakeholder impacts, and AI evaluation.  
**Caution:** No checklist alone establishes fairness; remedies require domain-specific evidence.

### C3. UNESCO (2021), Recommendation on the Ethics of Artificial Intelligence
https://www.unesco.org/en/artificial-intelligence/recommendation-ethics  
**Relevance:** Human rights, dignity, oversight, transparency, and social and environmental considerations.  
**Use:** Ethical review and governance of reflective-compute proposals.  
**Caution:** Translate principles into testable safeguards; a policy statement is not proof of compliance.

## D. Free software and reproducible computing

### D1. GNU Guix Manual
Official documentation: https://guix.gnu.org/manual/  
**Relevance:** Functional package management, declarative configuration, and build-oriented workflows.  
**Use:** Environment definitions, package provenance, and reproducibility experiments.  
**Caution:** A declared environment or successful build does not alone prove bit-for-bit reproducibility. Test the actual package, platform, inputs, and build process.

### D2. Reproducible Builds project
https://reproducible-builds.org/  
**Relevance:** Technical resources on producing bit-for-bit identical build outputs from specified inputs and on common sources of nondeterminism.  
**Use:** Build experiments, threat models, and verification plans.  
**Caution:** Reproducibility is not, by itself, proof of software correctness or security.

### D3. Linux kernel documentation
https://docs.kernel.org/  
**Relevance:** Version-specific technical documentation for kernel behaviour, interfaces, configuration, and development.  
**Use:** Support narrowly scoped technical claims.  
**Caution:** Cite the relevant subsystem and version; a landing page alone is not evidence for a specific implementation detail.

### D4. Open Source Initiative — The Open Source Definition
https://opensource.org/osd  
**Relevance:** Supports precise distinction between open source, free software, source-available software, and proprietary software.  
**Use:** Licensing and terminology.  
**Caution:** Inspect the actual licence and version; “source available” does not automatically mean open source or free software.

## E. African history and knowledge contexts

### E1. UNESCO, General History of Africa
https://www.unesco.org/en/general-history-africa  
**Relevance:** A multi-volume historical corpus developed with extensive African scholarly participation, covering methodology, prehistory, ancient civilisations, colonial history, and Africa and its diasporas.  
**Use:** Historical context and discovery of specialist bibliographies.  
**Caution:** Cite the specific volume, chapter, author, and page; this broad corpus is not a universal source for every science or technology claim.

### E2. Community-held and oral knowledge records
**Status:** Research method category; not a single publication.  
**Relevance:** Written archives do not exhaust how communities preserve knowledge.  
**Use:** Locate, document, and contextualise oral histories, language, craft, ecological knowledge, and situated technical practice.  
**Caution:** This is a research direction, not evidence for any specific tradition. Record provenance, permissions, attribution preferences, restrictions, and contested interpretations. Public discoverability does not imply unrestricted permission to reproduce.

## Extension protocol

For every source, use the source-annotation template. Verify bibliographic details and relevant passages against the source itself; identify the exact claim or question it informs; record limitations, disagreement, licensing, and access restrictions. A source listing is not proof that every claim in that source is correct.
